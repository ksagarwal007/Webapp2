Rent or buy a home in India? How to decide, with a worked example

Buying feels like building an asset and renting feels like money gone. Both feelings are only half right. This guide shows how to compare the two honestly, using one salaried buyer's numbers, and what it takes for buying to come out ahead.

The short answer

Whether renting or buying leaves you richer comes down to three numbers: how long you'll live in the home, how fast home prices grow where you're buying, and what your money would earn if you invested it instead. Tax rules, stamp duty and EMIs matter too, but less than people expect.

With the kind of numbers common in big Indian cities today, renting and investing the difference often comes out ahead on money alone. Buying wins when you stay a long time, prices in your area rise strongly, and you'd otherwise invest cautiously. The rest of this guide shows why, step by step.

Check your own numbers

Is rent really wasted money?

Rent is money you never get back. But owners also pay money they never get back, and it's easy to forget how much:

  • Interest on the loan. In the example below, ₹61.4 L of the ₹90 L paid in EMIs over 12 years is interest. Only the rest reduces the loan.
  • Stamp duty and registration, paid once and gone: ₹6.6 L on a ₹90 L flat in the example.
  • Maintenance, property tax and repairs every year.
  • Selling costs and tax on the gain when you sell.
  • What the down payment could have earned if it had been invested instead.

So the fair question isn't "rent or EMI?" but "after all these costs, which choice leaves me with more?" That's what the calculator answers.

Meet Arjun: the example

Arjun is 31, works in IT in Pune and earns ₹18 lakh a year. He files under the new tax regime, where his top slab is 20%. He's choosing between buying a 2BHK and renting a similar flat. Arjun is an example: his numbers are realistic, but use your own when you try the calculator.

Arjun's result, line by line

The verdict: Renting wins by ₹70.8 L

After 12 years, renting and investing leaves Arjun with ₹1.96 Cr after tax. Buying leaves him with ₹1.25 Cr once he sells the flat, pays 1% in selling costs and ₹10.6 L of tax on the gain, and repays the ₹43.4 L still owed on the loan.

How can that be, when the flat itself grows to ₹1.81 Cr? Because the renter starts with a ₹26.1 L head start (the down payment and buying costs, invested on day one) and keeps investing the gap between his rent and the buyer's EMI and upkeep. Arjun's rent starts at less than half his EMI, so that gap is large for years.

"Buying wins if prices grow above 9.4% a year"

This is the breakeven: the yearly home price growth at which both choices end level. It's the most useful number on the page. Ask yourself honestly whether homes in your area are likely to rise faster than that, every year, for your whole stay. If the breakeven is well above what you expect, renting is the safer bet. If it's below, buying looks good.

"Buying pays off: not within 30 years"

This tells you how long you'd need to stay for buying to come out ahead. For Arjun, buying never catches up at these numbers, even over 30 years. When buying does win, this number tells you the minimum stay that makes it worth it.

Cash needed to buy: ₹26.1 L

Down payment plus stamp duty, registration and other costs. This is real money that leaves your savings on day one, and it's exactly what the renter invests instead.

The chart and "Where your money went"

The chart shows what each choice would be worth if you cashed out in any given month. The buyer's line starts lower, because stamp duty and buying costs are gone the moment you buy. The table below it lists every rupee: what each choice paid, what it invested and what it ends with. Both choices use exactly the same money, so the comparison is fair.

Two things stand out. Home price growth moves the result more than anything else, and the breakeven barely moves when tax or the investment type changes. Stay length matters too: here a longer stay widens the gap, because Arjun's equity investments compound faster than a flat growing at 6%.

When buying wins

Change three things: prices in Arjun's area grow 8% a year, he'd only expect 10% a year from investing, and he stays 15 years and keeps the flat. Now Buying wins by ₹26.5 L, and buying pays off after about 5.6 years.

That's the pattern: buying wins when you stay long, local prices rise strongly and steadily, and your alternative investments would grow slowly. If those describe you, buying can make sense on money alone.

The catch with renting and investing

Renting only wins if you actually invest the difference, every month, for years, and leave it alone when markets fall. Many people don't. If the money saved by renting would drift into spending, the EMI works as forced saving and buying may leave you better off in practice.

A practical test: set up a monthly SIP for the gap between your EMI and rent for six months before deciding. If it feels easy, the renting numbers are realistic for you.

Old regime, HRA and home loan tax benefits

Under the old regime, a buyer can deduct up to ₹2,00,000 of home loan interest a year on a home they live in (section 22 of the Income-tax Act, 2025), and count principal, stamp duty and registration within the ₹1,50,000 limit of section 123. A renter can claim the HRA exemption instead. The new regime allows neither.

These benefits help both sides, so they move the answer less than people think. For Arjun on the old regime, with a basic salary of ₹60,000 a month and HRA of ₹30,000, the result is: Renting wins by ₹73.9 L, close to his new-regime result. What the numbers can't capture

A calculator compares money. Your decision is about more than that:

  • Reasons to buy anyway: no landlord and no forced moves, stability for children's schooling or ageing parents, freedom to change the home, and a home that's yours in retirement.
  • Reasons to keep renting: freedom to move for a better job, money that stays liquid for emergencies and opportunities, and no risk from a delayed project or a slow resale market.

It's fine to buy even when renting wins on paper. Just know what the peace of mind is costing you, and decide with that number in front of you.

A checklist before you decide

  1. How long will you realistically live in this home? Be honest about job moves.
  2. How have home prices in this specific area moved over the last 10 years?
  3. After the down payment and costs, will you still have an emergency fund of 6 months' expenses?
  4. Could you keep paying the EMI if your income dropped for a few months?
  5. If you rent, will you really invest the difference every month?
  6. Under construction? Add a year or two to the builder's possession date and check again.

How these numbers were worked out

Every figure in this guide comes from the same calculation as the Rent or Buy calculator, run on Arjun's inputs, so the guide and the tool always agree. The calculation runs month by month, with the same money going into both choices. It includes stamp duty, registration, EMIs, owner's costs, rent, the security deposit, investment tax at the end and tax on the home sale. The tax rules are for tax year 2026-27: gains on a home held over 24 months are taxed at 12.5% plus cess without indexation, and equity fund gains above ₹1,25,000 at 12.5% plus cess.
