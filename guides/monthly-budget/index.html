How to make a monthly budget on a salary in India, with a worked example

A budget isn't about tracking every rupee. It's about knowing, before the month starts, what's coming in, what has to go out, and what you'll keep. Here's a simple way to do it on a salary, worked through one family's real-looking numbers.

The short answer

  1. Start from your in-hand salary, not your CTC. That's the money you actually have.
  2. List every cost, including the yearly ones, and turn each yearly cost into a monthly amount to put aside.
  3. Decide your savings first and set them to leave your account right after salary day.
  4. Spend what's left, and check the plan once a month.

The budget planner does the arithmetic as you type. The rest of this guide shows what the numbers mean, using one family's budget.

Meera's budget: the example

Meera is 34 and lives in Pune with her husband and their 7-year-old. Her in-hand salary is ₹1,10,000 a month, plus a yearly bonus of ₹1,20,000. Her husband's income covers other family goals, so this budget runs on her pay. She has ₹2,00,000 set aside for emergencies. Meera is an example; use your own numbers in the planner.

Her result, line by line

Money left each month: ₹7,933

Her income averages ₹1,20,000 a month once the bonus is spread over 12 months. Needs come to ₹78,733, wants to ₹18,333, and she puts ₹15,000 into SIPs. That leaves ₹7,933.

Savings rate: 19%

Her SIPs plus what's left over, as a share of her income. It's the single best measure of whether a budget builds wealth. Many planners suggest aiming for 20% or more; Meera is just short.

EMI load: 11%

Her car EMI of ₹12,000 is 11% of her in-hand salary. Lenders look at this share before giving a new loan, and a low figure leaves room if her income dips.

Emergency fund: 2.5 months

Her ₹2,00,000 would cover 2.5 months of needs, including the EMI. The usual target is 6 months, so this is her weakest spot.

Set aside for yearly costs: ₹17,917 a month

School fees, the health insurance premium, festivals and the family holiday add up to ₹2,15,000 a year. Spread evenly, that's ₹17,917 a month she needs to put aside, even in months when nothing is due.

The bonus trap

Meera's budget only works because of her bonus. Spread over the year, it adds ₹10,000 a month. Without it, she'd be short by ₹2,067 a month.

That's common, and risky: bonuses and variable pay can shrink in a bad year. A safer plan covers needs and regular savings from the fixed salary, and uses the bonus for yearly costs, the emergency fund or extra investing. In the planner, compare your regular income with your total to see how much your plan leans on the bonus.

Needs, wants and savings

The 50/30/20 rule suggests about half of take-home pay for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for savings. Meera's split is 66% needs, 15% wants and 19% savings.

Her needs are well above 50%, mostly rent and school fees, and that's normal for a family in a big Indian city. The rule is a starting point, not a test you pass or fail. The planner lets you set a target that fits your life and shows where you are against it.

Yearly costs: the budget breakers

Most budgets don't fail on groceries. They fail in June when school fees are due, or in October when Diwali spending arrives on top of a normal month. The fix is simple:

  • List every yearly cost: school fees, insurance premiums, vehicle insurance and servicing, festivals, weddings, holidays, gadgets.
  • Divide each by 12. The planner does this and shows the monthly total.
  • Move that amount every month to a separate savings account or recurring deposit, and pay the bills from there when they come.

Small changes, real difference

If Meera trims eating out from ₹6,000 to ₹4,000 and shopping from ₹4,000 to ₹3,000, she keeps ₹10,933 a month instead of ₹7,933, and her savings rate rises to 22%. That's ₹36,000 more a year, from two lines.

Wants are the easiest place to start because they're choices. Needs take longer to change: a cheaper home or school is a big decision, not a monthly tweak.

Building the emergency fund

Six months of Meera's needs is about ₹4,72,400. She has ₹2,00,000, so she's ₹2,72,400 short. Putting all of her monthly surplus towards it would take about 34 months, or about 25 months with the trims above. Using part of her next bonus would close the gap much faster.

Keep the emergency fund where you can reach it within a day or two, like a savings account or a liquid fund, and don't count long-term investments towards it.

Habits that make a budget stick

  1. Pay yourself first: set SIPs to go out the day after salary day.
  2. Give yearly costs their own account, topped up every month.
  3. Build the emergency fund before investing for growth.
  4. Plan bigger spends ahead, like holidays around 2027's long weekends.
  5. Review once a month: save your budget from the planner, load it next month and update what changed.

How these numbers were worked out

Every figure in this guide comes from the same calculation as the budget planner, run on Meera's numbers. Yearly amounts are divided by 12, and daily amounts are multiplied by 365 and divided by 12. The savings rate counts planned savings plus what's left over, as a share of total income with the bonus spread monthly. The planner works with in-hand pay, after tax, so no tax rules apply.
