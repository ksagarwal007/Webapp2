Prepay your home loan or invest? How to decide, with a worked example

Got some spare money each month and a home loan? You can pay the loan off faster, or invest the money and let the loan run. Both feel right. This guide shows how to compare them properly, after tax, using one borrower's numbers.

The short answer

Prepaying your home loan earns you a guaranteed return equal to your loan rate, with no tax on it. Investing can earn more, but it isn't guaranteed, and the gains are taxed. So the question is simple: after tax, will your investments beat your loan rate, reliably, for years?

If you'd invest in equity funds for the long run, investing usually wins at today's loan rates. If you'd only use fixed deposits or debt funds, prepaying usually wins. If you're on the old tax regime and claim home loan interest, investing gets a further edge.

Check your own numbers

Meet Rohan: the example

Rohan is 36 and lives in Bengaluru. He owes ₹40,00,000 on his home loan at 8.5%, with 15 years left, so his EMI is ₹39,390. After a raise, he has ₹15,000 a month spare. He's on the new tax regime with a 30% top slab, and if he invests, it'll be equity mutual funds, where he expects about 12% a year over the long run. Rohan is an example; use your own numbers in the calculator.

Rohan's result, line by line

The verdict: Investing wins by ₹7.6 L

Both choices put the same money to work every month for 15 years. If Rohan prepays, his loan closes in 8.8 years instead of 15; after that, his EMI and the spare ₹15,000 both go into equity funds. If he invests from the start, the loan runs its full course while his SIP grows.

After 15 years and after tax, investing leaves him with ₹65.2 L, and prepaying with ₹57.6 L.

Interest saved by prepaying: ₹14.2 L

Prepaying cuts his total interest from ₹30.9 L to ₹16.7 L. That's a real, guaranteed saving, and it's why prepaying feels so good. But it isn't the whole answer: the verdict also counts what the same money would have earned if invested.

"Investing wins if your return beats 9.6% a year"

This is the breakeven: the yearly investment return at which both choices end level. For Rohan it's 9.6%, a little above his 8.5% loan rate, because investment gains are taxed and loan savings aren't. Ask yourself honestly whether your investments will beat that, year after year.

Why prepaying is a guaranteed return

Every rupee you prepay stops costing you 8.5% a year in interest. That's the same as earning 8.5% on it, with no market risk and no tax. Few safe investments match that today, which is why prepaying beats fixed deposits for most borrowers.

Equity funds have historically done better over long periods, but they go up and down, and some years they fall. The calculator assumes a steady return, so treat its answer as what happens if your expectation comes true.

The investment return decides most of it. Safe options like FDs and debt funds lose clearly to prepaying, because their returns are close to the loan rate and fully taxed. A higher loan rate helps prepaying, and a shorter remaining loan shrinks the difference to almost nothing.

The old regime changes the maths

Under the old regime, you can deduct up to ₹2,00,000 a year of interest on a home you live in (section 22 of the Income-tax Act, 2025). Prepaying cuts your interest, so it also cuts this tax saving. For Rohan, switching to the old regime and claiming interest moves the result to Investing wins by ₹13.3 L, and the breakeven return drops to 7.6%.

In the first years of a big loan the interest is well above ₹2,00,000 a year, so small prepayments barely touch the deduction at first. The calculator works this out year by year.

What the numbers can't capture

  • Reasons to prepay anyway: being debt-free sooner, a lower EMI burden if your income drops, and peace of mind that no market can take away.
  • Reasons to invest: money you can reach in an emergency (prepaid money is locked in the house), and a bigger pot if markets do well.

Check your emergency fund first. Money prepaid into a home loan is hard to get back when you need it.

You don't have to choose just one

Many people split the money: some into the loan, some into a SIP. It's a sensible hedge when you're unsure about returns. Run the calculator with your full spare amount, then with half, and compare. Also check your loan's terms: most floating-rate home loans for individuals have no prepayment charges, but fixed-rate loans can, so ask your lender.

A checklist before you decide

  1. Is your emergency fund in place (about 6 months of expenses)?
  2. What's your loan rate today, and is it fixed or floating?
  3. Where would you really invest the money, and what return is realistic after tax?
  4. Are you on the old or new tax regime, and do you claim home loan interest?
  5. Would you actually keep investing every month, even when markets fall?
  6. Does your lender charge for prepayment?
